The problem

Every AI product in the operations category sells the same thing: a chat box that promises to do your work. Ops leads don't buy it — not because the models are weak, but because a prompt has no permissions, no escalation path, and no record of what it did. The blocker isn't capability, it's accountability. Nobody signs off on automation they can't audit.

Who it's for

Operations, finance, and supply-chain teams at companies where a missed reconciliation costs real money — the people who close books, chase vendors, and answer to auditors. They already know which tasks are mechanical. What they need is a way to hand those tasks off that survives a compliance review.

The product

Nexum reframes the unit of automation from the prompt to the worker: a scoped operator that owns one job, holds its own permissions, and writes to an audit trail. Six modules — Intake Router, Ledger Sync, Escalation Guard, Vendor Comms, Audit Trail, Shift Handoff — compose into a runtime you turn on a piece at a time.

The Escalation Guard carries the argument. It holds a confidence threshold and hands back anything below it, so the product's honest claim is 97%, not 100%. Solutions are scoped by department rather than sold as one horizontal promise, and pricing is per worker rather than per seat — the whole team can watch the workers run and you only pay for the workers.

Key decisions

Lead with the ledger, not the chat box: the hero surface shows completed actions with timestamps and counts, because evidence sells to this buyer where a demo prompt doesn't. Make the limit a feature — the threshold that wakes a human is the most reassuring thing on the page, so it gets its own module instead of a footnote. Price per worker to make the value legible against a headcount line. And hold the whole site to an austere monochrome: near-black surface, hairline rules, one weight of white. Ops software earns trust by looking like infrastructure, not like a toy.

Outcome

The narrative moves a visitor from "another AI wrapper" to "a system with a paper trail" before the first scroll ends. Modules give a hesitant buyer a small first commitment, the department-scoped solutions answer "what ships in month one," and the audit trail answers the question that actually kills these deals. The headline result the product claims — a nine-day monthly close collapsing to two — reads as a consequence of the structure rather than a marketing number.

Reflection

Designing for AI adoption turned out to be less about showing intelligence and more about showing restraint. Every element that admitted a limit — the confidence threshold, the 3% handed back, the 99.2% rather than 100% — made the product more believable, not less. Trust is built out of the things a system refuses to do on its own.
